\chapter{Literature Review}\label{chap:LiteratureReview}

% DRAFT: reword in my own words before submission
Federated learning (FL) trains a shared model without moving raw data off the devices that produced it. Only model updates are exchanged, which is why FL is attractive for privacy-sensitive Internet of Things (IoT) applications. The problem is that IoT clients are usually too weak, and too far from the cloud, to run FL efficiently by themselves. Fog and edge computing address this by placing servers close to the devices, where updates can be aggregated and training tasks can run. These servers have limited resources, and they are shared. More clients mean more communication, more computation and, when privacy protection is added, more overhead on top of that. How well fog-based FL scales therefore depends to a large extent on how the fog layer allocates its resources.

This chapter reviews seventeen papers retrieved from IEEE Xplore, Scopus and ResearchGate, spanning the hardware, software and measurement sides of the topic. Section~\ref{sec:lit-architecture} covers hierarchical and decentralized FL architectures at the edge and fog, with a short overview of privacy-preserving mechanisms. Section~\ref{sec:lit-heterogeneity} turns to statistical heterogeneity, model optimization and the limits of IoT edge devices. Section~\ref{sec:lit-autoscaling} reviews proactive autoscaling of containerized workloads, the line of work this thesis builds on. Section~\ref{sec:lit-gap} compares the papers and states the research gap.

\section{Architectural Hierarchy, Decentralization and Edge/Fog Computing}\label{sec:lit-architecture}

Liu et al.~\cite{liu2020client} introduced the three-tier client-edge-cloud architecture that much of the later work builds on. Their algorithm, Hierarchical Federated Averaging (HierFAVG), lets edge servers aggregate nearby client models often while the cloud aggregates the edge models only occasionally, which keeps most traffic off the wide area network. On MNIST and CIFAR-10 this shortened training and reduced client energy use compared with cloud-only FL. The experiments assume identical channels and computing capacity for every client, and the edge servers' resources stay fixed throughout, so the question of how the edge layer should share resources under changing load never comes up.

Qayyum et al.~\cite{qayyum2025dhfl} go a step further with DHFL and remove the cloud from global aggregation entirely. Each round, particle swarm optimization picks one fog node as the global aggregator, weighing data quality, workload, communication cost and availability; the cloud receives only small heartbeat messages. On a multi-region cloud testbed, DHFL achieved higher accuracy and a much lower per-round communication delay than static hierarchical FL. What DHFL decides is where aggregation happens, not how much compute the chosen node gets.

AutoEdge, by Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao_autoedge}, is one of the few works here that changes resources over time. It decides online how many edge servers to rent for hierarchical FL on public edge platforms, balancing rental and switching costs against uncertain demand and using randomized rounding to arrive at whole servers. In a trace-driven simulation built on London Underground passenger traffic, it cost less than greedy and lazy-switching baselines. The granularity is coarse, though: entire server instances in fixed time slots, evaluated only in simulation, with no adjustment of the CPU given to FL workers while they run.

Prigent et al.~\cite{prigent2024continuum} survey FL across the edge-to-cloud continuum, drawing on 53 studies and 20 open-source frameworks such as Flower and FedScale. They sort the open problems into statistical and system heterogeneity, client volatility, security, and large-scale orchestration. Two of their observations matter here: most studies are evaluated only in simulation, and most report training rounds rather than the resources actually consumed. Being a survey, the paper identifies these gaps without working on them, and run-time compute allocation for FL is not discussed.

\subsection{Privacy-Preserving Mechanisms}\label{sec:lit-privacy}

Keeping data local does not by itself make FL private, because shared model updates can still leak information about the training data. Several of the reviewed papers address this. Khan et al.~\cite{khan2025wearable} and Dinh et al.~\cite{dinh_ckks} encrypt updates with CKKS homomorphic encryption so they can be aggregated while still encrypted. Huang et al.~\cite{huang2025ceppfl} combine differential privacy with compressed sensing at the fog nodes, and Niu et al.~\cite{niu2025verifiable} split gradients via secret sharing across two non-colluding servers, which also lets the aggregation be verified. Valdez-Solis et al.~\cite{valdezsolis2026six_g} survey how fog computing, blockchain and FL can jointly secure 6G networks. Every one of these mechanisms costs something. In the experiments of Dinh et al., encrypted updates grew large enough that fewer clients met the round deadline, and accuracy dropped as a result. This thesis does not deal with cryptographic privacy, but that added load on fog nodes is one more reason to allocate fog resources adaptively.

\section{Statistical Heterogeneity, Model Optimization and Constraints at the IoT Edge}\label{sec:lit-heterogeneity}

Ramadan et al.~\cite{ramadan2025tinyml} look at the very bottom of the hierarchy: FL combined with TinyML on microcontroller-class devices. They review the area and propose a framework with over-the-air updates, LoRa links and lossless compression, in which Raspberry Pi nodes aggregate models from clients such as the ESP32. Compression cuts both the data clients send and the power they use. The authors are open about large-scale deployment still being largely theoretical, and their optimizations target the clients and the radio link rather than the aggregation layer above them.

Jalali KhalilAbadi et al.~\cite{jalali2026cef} cover far more ground, surveying 115 studies on FL across cloud, edge and fog and organizing them into eight research directions ranging from security and privacy to resource scheduling. Their trade-off matrix of privacy techniques is a useful reference, and, like Prigent et al., they find the field leans heavily on simulation. Resource scheduling appears as one category among eight, without any analysis of how the compute of fog FL workloads should change over time.

Liu et al.~\cite{liu2023quantization} later extended HierFAVG with low-bit quantization of model updates. They prove convergence of Q-HierFAVG for non-convex models and derive how to pick aggregation intervals and bit widths, showing that network traffic can be cut without losing target accuracy. The analysis relies on static channels and bounded gradients, and, as in the original HierFAVG, communication is treated as the bottleneck while edge compute stays fixed.

Kotecha et al.~\cite{kotecha2024performance} review FL frameworks for edge computing and include a FedAvg case study on MNIST digit recognition, where a two-layer network reaches high accuracy within a few rounds. With such a small model, lightly shuffled data and no physical network, the study offers little insight into larger models or contended edge resources.

Abdel-Basset et al.~\cite{abdelbasset2024ppfdl} tackle non-IID data and privacy together in fog-assisted IoT with PP-FDL. A generative adversarial network at each fog node produces private class identifiers that prevent attackers from recovering the node's classes, and the models are combined with FedProx. On MNIST and CIFAR-10, PP-FDL resisted inference attacks and outperformed standard FL on non-IID data while avoiding the cost of encryption. The generative network is extra work at every fog node, however, and with only ten virtual clients the evaluation cannot show how that cost grows with scale.

\section[Proactive Autoscaling for Containerized Workloads]{Proactive Autoscaling for Containerized\\Workloads}\label{sec:lit-autoscaling}

The closest work to this thesis is FedAvg-BiGRU by Dogani and Khunjush~\cite{dogani2024}, a proactive autoscaler for Kubernetes at the edge. A BiGRU workload predictor is trained with FL across edge nodes, so the cloud receives model updates rather than raw workload data. A planner turns the multi-step forecasts into a pod count and uses a Cool Down Time (CDT) to prevent contradictory scaling actions. In simulation on the FIFA World Cup request traces, it under-provisioned less than the reactive Kubernetes autoscaler, at the price of more over-provisioning. Two points matter for this thesis. What gets scaled is the pods of a containerized web application, with FL used only to train the predictor, and CPU allocation for containers that themselves run FL is not considered.

Nikolaidis et al.~\cite{nikolaidis2023} approach the problem from the other side, asking how CPU limits affect FL containers. Running Flower on a four-node physical Kubernetes cluster, they found that default orchestrator settings fit FL workloads poorly. One client used only about nine of its sixteen reserved CPUs, yet halving the reservation to eight CPUs made training about 22\% slower. They also note that FL workloads follow predictable performance patterns, which is what a proactive controller depends on. Their limits, though, are set before training and never changed; a module that adjusts them at run time is left as future work.

Subramanya and Riggio~\cite{subramanya2021} connect forecasting and scaling in a 5G setting. They train FFNN, LSTM and CNN-LSTM models, both centrally and with FL, on 43 days of hourly traffic from three base stations, and use the forecasts to scale an nginx server on a Kubernetes prototype, horizontally by instance and vertically in 100-millicore steps. Predictive scaling responded faster than the reactive Kubernetes baseline, which waited three to five minutes before reacting to traffic spikes. The federated predictors were less accurate than the centralized ones (for the LSTM, a mean absolute error of 1.49 versus 0.55), which the authors attribute to non-IID data. Two limitations stand out for this thesis: the scaled application is a web server rather than an FL job, and every vertical change is applied by redeploying, which restarts the pod and increases response time at load peaks.

Reading across the table, three things become clear. The architectural papers lower the cost of FL by restructuring the system, whether by aggregating at the edge~\cite{liu2020client}, moving global aggregation into the fog~\cite{qayyum2025dhfl} or sending fewer bits~\cite{liu2023quantization}, and all of them take the compute of each tier as given. Evaluation is mostly simulation-based: both surveys report this for the majority of their studies~\cite{prigent2024continuum,jalali2026cef}, and it holds for most papers here as well, including Dogani and Khunjush, whose CPU load is simulated~\cite{dogani2024}. Finally, the papers that do adapt resources either scale something other than FL, namely web servers and virtual network functions~\cite{subramanya2021,dogani2024}, or scale FL coarsely, such as whole edge servers in 15-minute slots~\cite{zhao_autoedge}. The only study that examines the CPU of FL containers directly~\cite{nikolaidis2023} fixes it before training and defers run-time adjustment to future work.


Taken together, the pieces exist but have not been combined. Proactive, forecast-driven autoscaling reduces under-provisioning for web workloads, and FL workloads are known to be sensitive to their CPU allocation and to behave predictably. No reviewed work, however, proactively reallocates the CPU of running FL worker containers in a fog layer while measuring how this affects the FL system itself.

This thesis works toward closing that gap in two phases. The first reproduces the FedAvg-BiGRU autoscaler of Dogani and Khunjush~\cite{dogani2024} on the same dataset, but replaces the simulated CPU load with real Docker containers monitored through Prometheus and Grafana, and measures the data transfer that the original paper reports without measuring. The second applies proactive allocation to the FL workers themselves, changing their CPU limits at run time without restarting them, in contrast to the redeployment-based vertical scaling of Subramanya and Riggio~\cite{subramanya2021}.
