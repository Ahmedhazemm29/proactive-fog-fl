\chapter{Literature Review}\label{chap:LiteratureReview}

% DRAFT: reword in my own words before submission
Federated learning (FL) trains a shared model while the raw data stays on the devices that produced it. Only model updates leave a device, which makes FL attractive for privacy-sensitive Internet of Things (IoT) applications. In practice, the clients are often too weak and too far from the cloud to run FL efficiently on their own. Fog and edge computing place an intermediate layer of servers close to the devices, where model updates can be aggregated and training tasks can run. This layer, however, has limited and shared resources. As the number of clients grows, the fog layer must handle more communication, more computation and the extra overhead of privacy protection, so the scalability of fog-based FL depends heavily on how its resources are allocated.

This chapter reviews seventeen papers found on IEEE Xplore, Scopus and ResearchGate, covering the hardware, software and measurement aspects of the topic. They are grouped into three themes. Section~\ref{sec:lit-architecture} covers hierarchical and decentralized FL architectures at the edge and fog, including a short overview of privacy-preserving mechanisms. Section~\ref{sec:lit-heterogeneity} covers statistical heterogeneity, model optimization and the constraints of IoT edge devices. Section~\ref{sec:lit-autoscaling} covers proactive autoscaling of containerized workloads, which is the starting point of this thesis. Section~\ref{sec:lit-gap} compares the papers and identifies the research gap that this thesis addresses.

\section{Architectural Hierarchy, Decentralization and Edge/Fog Computing}\label{sec:lit-architecture}

Liu et al.~\cite{liu2020client} propose a three-tier client-edge-cloud architecture for federated learning, trained with Hierarchical Federated Averaging (HierFAVG). Edge servers aggregate the models of nearby clients frequently, while the cloud aggregates the edge models only occasionally, so fewer models cross the wide area network. In experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10, this shortens training time and lowers client energy consumption compared with cloud-only federated learning. However, the authors assume identical channels and computing capacity for all clients and treat the resources of the edge servers as fixed. They do not consider how the edge layer should share its resources when the load changes.

Qayyum et al.~\cite{qayyum2025dhfl} propose DHFL, which moves global aggregation from the cloud to the fog layer. In each round, a particle swarm optimization selects the fog node that acts as the global aggregator, based on data quality, workload, communication cost and availability, so the cloud only receives small heartbeat messages instead of full models. On a multi-region cloud testbed, DHFL improves accuracy and greatly reduces the communication delay per round compared with static hierarchical federated learning. However, DHFL only decides where aggregation runs. It does not change how much compute the selected fog node receives.

Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao_autoedge} propose AutoEdge, which decides online how many edge servers to rent for hierarchical federated learning on public edge platforms. An online algorithm balances rental and switching costs against uncertain future demand, and randomized rounding turns its result into whole servers. In a trace-driven simulation based on London Underground passenger traffic, AutoEdge lowers the total cost compared with greedy and lazy-switching baselines. However, it scales whole server instances in coarse time slots and is evaluated only in simulation. It does not adjust the CPU of federated learning workers while they run.

Prigent et al.~\cite{prigent2024continuum} survey federated learning across the edge-to-cloud computing continuum, covering 53 studies and 20 open-source frameworks such as Flower and FedScale. They group the open challenges into statistical and system heterogeneity, client volatility, security and large-scale orchestration. They also find that most studies are evaluated only in simulation and report training rounds rather than the resources consumed. However, as a survey, the paper names these gaps without addressing them. It does not discuss how compute should be allocated to federated learning workloads at run time.

\subsection{Privacy-Preserving Mechanisms}

Keeping data local does not make federated learning fully private, since shared model updates can still leak information about the training data. Khan et al.~\cite{khan2025wearable} and Dinh et al.~\cite{dinh_ckks} encrypt the updates with CKKS homomorphic encryption, so they can be aggregated without being decrypted. Huang et al.~\cite{huang2025ceppfl} combine differential privacy with compressed sensing at fog nodes, and Niu et al.~\cite{niu2025verifiable} use secret sharing across two non-colluding servers to keep gradients confidential and to verify the aggregation. Valdez-Solis et al.~\cite{valdezsolis2026six_g} survey how fog computing, blockchain and federated learning can secure future 6G networks. All of these mechanisms add overhead: in the experiments of Dinh et al., the encrypted updates were so large that fewer clients met the round deadline, which lowered accuracy. However, cryptographic privacy is outside the scope of this thesis. The extra load it places on fog nodes strengthens the case for allocating fog resources adaptively.

\section{Statistical Heterogeneity, Model Optimization and Constraints at the IoT Edge}\label{sec:lit-heterogeneity}

Ramadan et al.~\cite{ramadan2025tinyml} review federated learning and TinyML on microcontroller-class IoT devices and propose a framework with over-the-air updates, LoRa communication and lossless compression. Raspberry Pi nodes aggregate the models of microcontroller clients such as the ESP32. Compression reduces the data that clients send and the power they consume. However, the authors admit that large-scale deployments remain mostly theoretical. The work optimizes the clients and the link, not the resources of the aggregation layer above them.

Jalali KhalilAbadi et al.~\cite{jalali2026cef} survey federated learning across cloud, edge and fog based on 115 studies, and organize the field into eight research directions, from security and privacy to resource scheduling. They compare privacy techniques in a trade-off matrix and, like Prigent et al., find that most work relies on simulation. However, resource scheduling is treated as one category among many. The survey does not analyze how the compute of federated learning workloads in the fog should change over time.

Liu et al.~\cite{liu2023quantization} extend HierFAVG with low-bit quantization of the model updates. They prove that the resulting Q-HierFAVG algorithm converges for non-convex models and show how to choose the aggregation intervals and the number of bits. Quantization reduces network traffic without lowering the target accuracy. However, the analysis assumes static channels and bounded gradients. Like its predecessor, the work treats communication as the bottleneck and the compute of the edge layer as fixed.

Kotecha et al.~\cite{kotecha2024performance} review federated learning frameworks for edge computing and present a FedAvg case study on handwritten digit recognition with MNIST. A small two-layer network reaches high accuracy within a few rounds while the raw data stays on the clients. However, the case study uses a very small model, lightly shuffled data and no physical network. It says little about federated learning with larger models or with shared, constrained edge resources.

Abdel-Basset et al.~\cite{abdelbasset2024ppfdl} propose PP-FDL, which handles non-IID data and privacy together in fog-assisted IoT. Each fog node uses a generative adversarial network to create private class identifiers that stop attackers from recovering its classes, and the models are combined with FedProx. On MNIST and CIFAR-10, the framework resists inference attacks and outperforms standard federated learning on non-IID data, without the cost of encryption. However, the generative network adds work at every fog node. The evaluation with ten virtual clients does not show how this cost grows as the system scales.

\section[Proactive Autoscaling for Containerized Workloads]{Proactive Autoscaling for Containerized\\Workloads}\label{sec:lit-autoscaling}

Dogani and Khunjush~\cite{dogani2024} propose FedAvg-BiGRU, a proactive autoscaler for Kubernetes at the edge. Federated learning trains a BiGRU workload predictor across edge nodes, so only model updates travel to the cloud instead of raw workload data. The planner then converts multi-step predictions into a number of pods, using the Cool Down Time (CDT) to avoid contradictory scaling actions. In simulation on FIFA World Cup request traces, the approach reduces under-provisioning compared with the reactive Kubernetes autoscaler, at the cost of more over-provisioning. However, the authors scale pods for containerized web applications and use federated learning only to train the predictor. They do not dynamically allocate CPU for containers running federated learning.

Nikolaidis et al.~\cite{nikolaidis2023} study how CPU limits affect federated learning containers running Flower on a Kubernetes cluster of four physical nodes. They show that default orchestrator settings are not suited to FL workloads: in their experiments, a client used about nine of sixteen reserved CPUs, and halving the reservation to eight CPUs increased training time by about 22\%. They also observe that FL workloads follow predictable performance patterns. However, the CPU limits are fixed before training starts, and a runtime module that adjusts them is left as future work.

Subramanya and Riggio~\cite{subramanya2021} train FFNN, LSTM and CNN-LSTM forecasting models, both centralized and federated, on 43 days of hourly traffic from three base stations, and use the predictions to scale an nginx web server on a Kubernetes prototype, horizontally in whole instances and vertically in steps of 100 millicores. Predictive scaling reacts faster than the Kubernetes reactive baseline, whose waits of three to five minutes delay its response to traffic spikes. The federated predictors are less accurate than the centralized ones; for the LSTM, the mean absolute error is 1.49 against 0.55, which the authors attribute to non-IID data. Critically for this thesis, the scaled application is a web server rather than an FL job, and vertical changes are applied through a new deployment that restarts the pod and raises response time at load peaks.

Three patterns stand out across the reviewed work. First, the architectural papers reduce the cost of FL by changing the structure of the system: aggregating at the edge~\cite{liu2020client}, moving global aggregation into the fog~\cite{qayyum2025dhfl}, or sending fewer bits~\cite{liu2023quantization}. They all assume that the compute available to each tier is fixed. Second, evaluation is dominated by simulation. The two surveys find this for most of their studies~\cite{prigent2024continuum,jalali2026cef}, and it also holds for most of the papers reviewed here, including the autoscaler of Dogani and Khunjush, whose CPU load is simulated~\cite{dogani2024}. Third, the papers that do adapt resources either scale something other than FL, namely web servers and virtual network functions~\cite{subramanya2021,dogani2024}, or scale FL at a coarse level, such as whole edge servers in 15-minute slots~\cite{zhao_autoedge}. The one study that looks at the CPU of FL containers directly fixes it before training and leaves run-time adjustment as future work~\cite{nikolaidis2023}.


The research gap therefore lies where these lines of work meet. Proactive, forecast-driven autoscaling has been shown to reduce under-provisioning for web workloads, and FL workloads have been shown to be sensitive to their CPU allocation and to follow predictable patterns. Yet no reviewed work proactively reallocates the CPU of running FL worker containers in a fog layer, while measuring the effect on the FL system itself. This thesis addresses that gap in two phases. It first reproduces the FedAvg-BiGRU autoscaler of Dogani and Khunjush~\cite{dogani2024} on the same dataset, replacing the simulated CPU load with real Docker containers, Prometheus and Grafana, and measuring the data transfer that the original paper only claims. It then applies proactive allocation to the FL workers themselves, changing their CPU limits at run time without restarting them, unlike the redeployment-based vertical scaling of Subramanya and Riggio~\cite{subramanya2021}.
